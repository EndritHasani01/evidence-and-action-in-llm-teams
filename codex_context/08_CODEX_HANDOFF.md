# Handoff prompt for the specification-writing session

You have the parent `recovery_blueprint` and this `codex_context` folder, plus web access. Your goal is to produce a single authoritative `PROJECT_SPECIFICATION.md` from which later research software, experiments, analysis, and writing can be created.

First understand the materials. Read the companion's authority rules and confirmed user context, then inventory and inspect every parent file relevant to scientific claims, contracts, budgets, schemas, reference code, and validation. Distinguish implemented artifacts from proposed ones. Treat older background reports as leads requiring verification.

Conduct an in-depth primary-source review of the latest relevant research and technologies. Resolve contradictions and prioritize close scientific overlap over broad tool lists. Record paper versions, documentation/release dates, exact repositories, licenses, and what you actually inspected. Do not infer absence from inaccessible sources. Obtain the remaining close paper if possible; otherwise make the limitation and its gate explicit.

Compare infrastructure candidates against the actual experiment. Respect the user's preference for established, maintained tools, and evaluate LangGraph as a leading candidate. Select only dependencies with a demonstrated purpose; do not prescribe a stack just because the old blueprint or this companion mentions it. Popularity alone does not establish correctness. Avoid unsupported “most used” rankings or guarantees of production reliability.

Preserve the research identity and explain any proposed change to the question, estimand, or design. Do not silently turn the study into a framework benchmark. Determine which choices can be finalized from evidence and which need a bounded pilot or user/supervisor decision. Carry out useful research and specification work before asking questions; ask only those whose answers materially change the result.

The final Markdown specification must be self-contained as the normative project contract. Include:

- Purpose, academic scope, research question, hypotheses, contribution boundaries, non-goals, and limitations.
- Traceable requirements with acceptance criteria, including observable-state and information-access contracts.
- Experimental factors, policies and controls, primary/secondary estimands, outcome definitions, population, exclusions, randomization, clustering, uncertainty, and negative-result handling.
- Selected tools and justified alternatives, version/compatibility strategy, data and interface contracts, provenance and reproducibility requirements, and minimum necessary innovations.
- Compute accounting, pilot gates, measured-versus-assumed values, stop/pivot rules, and staged deliverables.
- Validation obligations, framework-semantic risks, source/license constraints, open decisions, and change-control rules.
- Dated source references and an explicit reconciliation of conflicts with the input documents.

Use stable requirement and decision IDs. Make requirements concrete enough to validate, while avoiding arbitrary detail unsupported by research. The specification may cite external evidence, but should not require readers to resolve contradictory normative instructions scattered through the old files.

Do not manufacture test results, final versions, supervisor approval, or hardware measurements. Where feasibility requires a future test, state the test, pass criteria, and consequence of failure. Deliver the specification with its remaining gates clearly marked. Implementing the full system and running the main experiment are subsequent tasks, not part of this handoff's authorization.
